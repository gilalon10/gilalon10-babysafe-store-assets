How Hot Does a Parked Car Get? What Happens to a Child Inside, Minute by Minute

Every summer, parents search for the same thing: how hot can a car really get, and how fast? A closed car parked in the sun heats up very quickly, even when the weather outside feels pleasant, and a young child's body cannot keep up.

How fast does a car heat up? Minute by minute

In a study published in the journal Pediatrics in 2005, researchers including Jan Null of San José State University measured the inside of closed cars on days when it was between 72 and 96°F (22–36°C) outside. On average, the interior temperature rose by:

  • After 10 minutes: about 19°F (about 11°C)
  • After 20 minutes: about 29°F (about 16°C)
  • After 30 minutes: about 34°F (about 19°C)
  • After 60 minutes: about 43°F (about 24°C)
  • After 1 to 2 hours: about 45–50°F (about 25–28°C)

Most of the heat builds up in the first half hour

The researchers found that about 80 percent of the temperature rise happened within the first 30 minutes, and that the inside of a car heated up by an average of about 40°F (22°C) within an hour, regardless of the outside temperature. The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) puts it simply: a car can heat up by 20°F (about 11°C) in as little as 10 minutes.

A 2018 study by Arizona State University and UC San Diego, published in the journal Temperature, tested cars in Tempe, Arizona, on days above 100°F (38°C). After one hour in the sun, the cabin air averaged 116°F (47°C), the dashboard 157°F (69°C), the steering wheel 127°F (53°C) and the seats 123°F (51°C). Even in the shade, the cabin reached close to 100°F (38°C).

What happens to a child inside

Children are not small adults. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and NoHeatStroke.org, a child's body heats up 3 to 5 times faster than an adult's, and a child's temperature-regulating system is less efficient. Heatstroke begins when core body temperature goes above 104°F (40°C) and the body can no longer cool itself. At about 107°F (41.7°C), cells are damaged, organs begin to fail, and a child can die.

In the Arizona State study, a model of a 2-year-old reached a core temperature of 104°F (40°C) in about one hour in a car parked in the sun, and in just under two hours in the shade. The researchers note that the exact timing depends on the child's age, weight, health and clothing, and that harm can begin even below that point.

Is it dangerous even on a mild day?

Yes. NHTSA notes that the temperature inside a car can climb above 115°F (46°C) when it is only 70°F (21°C) outside, and that a child can die in a hot car even on a 60°F (16°C) day. The AAP says heatstroke can occur when it is only 57°F (14°C) outside.

Does cracking a window help?

No. In the Pediatrics study, cracking the windows changed the inside temperature by less than 3°F (under 2°C), and the researchers called the effect insignificant. NHTSA and the AAP agree that cracking a window, parking in the shade or running the air conditioner before you leave does little to protect a child inside. The only safe amount of time to leave a child alone in a car is none.

How often does this happen?

In the United States, NoHeatStroke.org reports an average of 37 child deaths per year from vehicular heatstroke since 1998. In more than half of the cases (52.9 percent), the child was forgotten by a caregiver, and in about 1 in 4 cases the child got into an unlocked car alone. Many of these parents were loving and attentive, but tired, distracted or out of their usual routine. It can happen to anyone.

If you see a child alone in a car

Act right away. Following NHTSA and AAP guidance:

  • Check whether the child is responsive.
  • Call your emergency number immediately (911 in the US, 112 in Europe and many other countries).
  • If the child is unresponsive or in distress, NHTSA advises getting into the car to help, even if that means breaking a window.
  • Once the child is out, cool them with cool water (not an ice bath) and stay with them until help arrives.
  • If the child is responsive, stay with them and have someone look for the driver.

Everyday habits that prevent it

Safety experts recommend a few simple routines:

  • Always look in the back seat before you lock the car and walk away. NHTSA's reminder: Stop. Look. Lock.
  • Put something you need, such as your phone or bag, in the back seat next to your child.
  • Keep a soft toy in the empty car seat and move it to the front seat whenever your child is in it.
  • Ask your daycare or babysitter to call you if your child is more than 10 minutes late.
  • Be extra careful when your routine changes, for example when a different parent does the drop-off.
  • Lock your car when it is parked, even at home, and keep keys out of children's reach.
  • Teach children that cars and trunks are not places to play.
