Why Loving Parents Forget a Child in the Car – and How to Protect Your Family

It is one of the hardest things to imagine: a devoted parent drives to work, parks and walks away, without realizing that their baby is still asleep in the back seat. Most of us are sure it could never happen to us. Memory research suggests otherwise, and understanding why is the first step toward preventing it.

This is sometimes called “forgotten baby syndrome.” It is not a medical diagnosis, and it is not a sign of neglect or a lack of love. It is a predictable weakness in the way the human brain handles memory, and it can be guarded against.

How often does it happen?

In the United States, meteorologist Jan Null of San José State University has tracked every child vehicular heatstroke death since 1998 at NoHeatStroke.org. From 1998 through 2025, 1,041 children died of heatstroke in vehicles, an average of about 37 a year. In 52.9% of those cases (551 children), the child had been forgotten by a caregiver. Most of the others involved children who got into a vehicle on their own (23.8%) or were knowingly left inside (21.9%).

Young children are especially vulnerable: according to the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), a child’s body temperature rises three to five times faster than an adult’s.

It can happen to anyone

Kids and Car Safety, a U.S. nonprofit, stresses that these tragedies happen regardless of education or socioeconomic status. The parents involved have included teachers, dentists, social workers, nurses, clergy, military officers and even a rocket scientist. In the words of neuroscientist David Diamond, it is “a matter of circumstances,” not of how much a parent cares.

Two memory systems in competition

David Diamond, a professor of psychology at the University of South Florida, has studied these cases for years, interviewing parents and reviewing brain research on memory. He describes the problem as a failure of prospective memory: the brain’s ability to remember to carry out a plan in the future, such as stopping at the store on the way home or dropping a child off at daycare.

According to Diamond, the frontal and parietal cortices form the plan, and the hippocampus helps us recall it at the right moment. But on a familiar drive, another system takes over: the basal ganglia, which run our habits on “autopilot.” This habit system lets us drive a well-known route almost without thinking. When the two systems compete, habit can win, and awareness of the plan, including the child in the back seat, can quietly slip away.

What makes a memory lapse more likely

Diamond’s research and Kids and Car Safety point to several common factors:

  • A change in the day’s routine, such as a holiday schedule or a different drop-off plan
  • Stress, which can make prospective memory fail within seconds
  • Sleep deprivation and fatigue, both very common for parents of young children
  • Distractions and multitasking, such as phone calls during a busy morning
  • A quiet child out of sight: a sleeping baby in a rear-facing seat gives no visual or acoustic reminder

Layer 1: everyday habits

The lesson is not to try harder to remember, but to stop relying on memory alone. Safety organizations including NHTSA and Kids and Car Safety recommend small habits that work even on your worst day:

  • Look before you lock: open the back door and check the back seat every time you park, even when you think the car is empty.
  • Put something you need in the back, such as your phone, bag, laptop or work badge, so you have to open the rear door before you can start your day.
  • Leave a cue up front: a diaper bag or stuffed animal on the passenger seat reminds you that your child is with you.
  • Agree on a call policy with your daycare or babysitter: they call you right away if your child has not arrived as expected, and you tell them when your child will be late or absent.
  • Never leave a child alone in a vehicle, even briefly. Cracked windows and shade do little to keep a car cool.

Layer 2: technology as a backup

Technology can add a second line of defense. In 2019, automakers in the U.S. voluntarily committed to fitting rear-seat reminder systems in virtually every new car by the 2025 model year. Many of these systems use “door logic”: if a rear door was opened before or during the trip, the car shows a message and sounds a chime when you switch it off. They do not detect a child; they simply prompt you to check.

The bottom line

Forgetting a child in a car says nothing about how much a parent loves their child. It reflects how human memory works under stress, fatigue and changing routines. By accepting that any of us can have a lapse, and by combining simple habits with reminders, every family can make checking the back seat automatic.
